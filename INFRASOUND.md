# Infrasound — assessment

Researched 2026-11-07. Expands [EXPANSION.md §6.5](EXPANSION.md).

**Summary up front:** infrasound is the only route to altitudes above the ~7 km propeller ceiling, and the
stratospheric sounding it enables is real, published science. But it is **a second instrument, not an
extension of PropDop** — it shares the mast, the power and the clock, and almost nothing else. And a
single sensor without wind-noise infrastructure and without an array delivers far less than the
literature's results imply.

---

## 1. Why a microphone cannot do this

Infrasound is below ~20 Hz, and a microphone is the wrong transducer for it.

Condenser and electret microphones are deliberately vented so static pressure equalises across the
diaphragm — otherwise weather and altitude would saturate them. That vent is a high-pass filter, typically
cornering around 10–20 Hz. The project's main concern (60–400 Hz blade-pass) sits comfortably above it;
infrasound sits below it.

The right sensor is a **differential pressure transducer** with one port open to the atmosphere and the
other behind a controlled leak into a reference volume. That leak sets the high-pass corner, and it can be
placed far lower than any microphone's.

The **Raspberry Shake BOOM** is the accessible version: a MEMS temperature-compensated differential
pressure transducer at 100 samples/s. Its spec sheet gives −3 dB points of **1 Hz to 44 Hz** with the
default 1-second mechanical filter, dropping to **0.08 Hz** with a 20-second filter; the manual quotes
roughly 0.05–20 Hz for infrasonic sources. The `RS&BOOM` variant pairs it with a seismometer, which
matters — see §5.

## 2. Wind noise is the whole problem, and the fix is large

Every infrasound source I read is blunt that wind dominates, and the engineering response is physically
big. These are measured numbers:

| Wind-noise reducer | Reduction | Band | Size |
|---|---|---|---|
| **18 m rosette** pipe filter | 15–20 dB | above 0.2 Hz, winds ≤5.5 m/s | 18 m across |
| **70 m rosette** (144 inlets) | up to 15–20 dB | 0.02–0.7 Hz | 70 m across |
| Wind barrier | 20–25 dB above its corner; only ~4 dB below | — | fenced enclosure |
| **OFIS** (optical fibre, spatial averaging along a line) | ~10 dB better than a reference at 1 Hz in 5 m/s wind | — | linear, land-efficient |

Three things follow:

- **Rosettes are 18–70 m structures.** This is the IMS standard, and it is not a garden installation. The
  70 m version is what buys the microbarom band (0.02–0.7 Hz).
- **Nothing helps below the corner frequency.** Rosettes give no reduction there, and barriers almost
  none. The lowest frequencies are simply wind-limited.
- **A bare sensor in the open will be wind-limited most of the time.** The same low-and-sheltered siting
  logic as the main project applies, but much more severely.

The one genuinely elegant data point: a **balloon-borne array at ~38 km** achieved near-total wind-noise
reduction simply by drifting *with* the wind. Not an option on a mast, but it shows how completely wind
sets the noise floor.

## 3. The big prize: stratospheric sounding from microbaroms

This is the scientifically strongest reason to care, and it reaches where nothing else in this project can.

**Microbaroms** are continuous infrasound near ~0.2 Hz generated by interacting ocean waves — a permanent,
free, global source. Infrasound at these frequencies refracts off the stratosphere and returns to the
ground at a few hundred km range, so its **travel times and arrival azimuths encode stratospheric wind and
temperature**.

What's established:

- Using continuous measurements to quantify stratospheric winds was **proposed over 40 years ago**; earlier
  inversions needed known transient sources such as large chemical explosions.
- Recent work shows **microbarom travel times through the stratosphere can be used to retrieve wind and
  temperature profiles** — this is the basis of the method (see the Eos piece *"Using Sounds from the Ocean
  to Measure Winds in the Stratosphere"*).
- **Back-azimuth deviations** are themselves a measurement: strong stratospheric winds systematically bias
  observed arrival azimuths.
- **It assimilates.** An ensemble-Kalman-filter study (arXiv 2004.07972) found infrasound observations
  "provide corrections to the wind in stratospheric and tropospheric altitudes" — and its authors
  explicitly flag continuous microbarom sources as the useful next step.
- Sudden stratospheric warmings have a detectable infrasonic signature (the 2009 major SSW).
- Wintertime stratospheric ducting from North Atlantic microbaroms is routinely observed at IMS arrays.

**Why this matters for this project's ambitions:** PropDop's acoustic sounding caps at ~7 km because
propellers are the only usable source ([RESEARCH.md §2.1](RESEARCH.md)). Infrasound jumps to the
**stratosphere, ~45–50 km** — an entirely different and much less observed altitude band, using a source
that never switches off.

**But the catch is severe.** Stratospheric retrieval needs:

- an **array**, for azimuth and wavefront speed (§4),
- **aperture matched to ~0.2 Hz**, where the wavelength is ~1.7 km — IMS arrays run 1–3 km apertures,
- **wind-noise reduction in the 0.02–0.7 Hz band**, i.e. the 70 m rosette class of infrastructure,
- and ideally other stations to correlate against.

That is a research-station build, not an add-on. The encouraging counter-example is the **NORSE
experiment**, which used *four-element* arrays alongside the IMS I37 array specifically to test noise
reduction and study stratospheric winds — so small arrays do real work when sited near a reference.

## 4. A single sensor gives you much less than the literature suggests

The same lesson as the microphone side of this project, and worth stating plainly: **one element has no
direction.**

- **One sensor**: event detection and arrival times only. You cannot tell a quarry blast to the north from
  thunder to the south.
- **Three or more non-collinear elements**: azimuth *and* wavefront trace velocity, which is what actually
  discriminates sources — a stratospheric return, a tropospheric duct and a local surface source have
  different apparent horizontal speeds. This is the measurement.
- Aperture scales with the band: ~100–300 m suffices for local sources at 1–10 Hz (λ = 34–343 m); ~1 km is
  wanted for microbaroms.

Timing is *not* the hard part, consistently with [DESIGN.md §4](DESIGN.md): across a 1 km aperture the
cross-array travel time is ~3 s, so millisecond-class sync — ordinary NTP, let alone GPS PPS — is ample.
**Land and wind-noise plumbing are the binding constraints, not clocks.**

An alternative worth knowing: a **line-based OFIS array** has been proposed as giving directional
resolution "requiring less space compared to an equally effective array of five microbarometers with
rosette wind filters." If land is the limit, that's the direction to read up on.

## 5. What a modest installation can actually detect

With one or a few elements, no rosettes, and realistic siting:

| Source | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Thunder and severe storms** | Strong, local, frequent. There's an active tornado-infrasound literature. Pairs well with an optical flash sensor ([EXPANSION.md §6.2](EXPANSION.md)) |
| **Sonic booms** | Rare, but **ADS-B makes them attributable** — the one place infrasound touches PropDop's existing machinery |
| **Bolides and meteors** | Large signals; pairs with an all-sky camera and the Global Meteor Network for optical-plus-acoustic trajectories |
| **Mining and quarry blasts** | Regular, repeatable, effectively a free calibration source if there's a quarry within range |
| **Earthquakes, seismo-acoustic coupling** | The `RS&BOOM` combination is genuinely useful: the seismometer tells you whether energy arrived through the ground or the air, which is the cleanest way to discriminate blasts from quakes from booms |
| **Wind turbines** | If any are nearby — blade-pass infrasound and amplitude modulation |
| **Gravity waves, pressure transients** | Needs the long-period filter configuration |
| **Helicopter main rotors** | ~10–20 Hz, so borderline — the only *aircraft* signal meaningfully in band |

Note what's **absent**: propeller blade-pass is 60–400 Hz, far above infrasound. So infrasound does
essentially nothing for the project's aircraft products. That's the crux of the honest assessment.

## 6. Honest assessment for this project

**Against:**

- It is **a separate instrument and a separate science.** It shares the site, power, network and clock, and
  shares the *wind-noise engineering mindset* — but none of the DSP, none of the ADS-B coupling, and none
  of the Doppler machinery. There is no synergy beyond the mast.
- The headline product (stratospheric sounding) needs an **array plus 70 m-class wind-noise reduction**.
  Without that you are not doing the thing the literature describes.
- PropDop has **not yet validated a single measurement.** Adding a second instrument with its own failure
  modes before the first works is how projects stall.

**For:**

- It is the **only path above 7 km**, and the stratosphere is genuinely under-observed — the assimilation
  literature says so explicitly.
- The source is **continuous and free**, unlike aircraft of opportunity.
- Even a single element joins an **established network** with real users, and the seismo-acoustic pairing
  is a legitimately useful discriminator.
- It's **immune to the thing that limits PropDop's atmospheric product** — it needs no overflying traffic,
  so it works in exactly the sparse-traffic sites where the aircraft method fails.

**Recommendation.** Treat it as a *later, separate* project rather than a PropDop expansion, and do not
start it until the aircraft pipeline has one validated product. When you do:

1. Start with a **single `RS&BOOM`** — seismo-acoustic discrimination, network participation, local event
   detection. Accept that there's no azimuth. A few hundred dollars and it tells you what your site's
   noise floor actually is, which is the information that decides everything after.
2. Spend the effort on **wind-noise reduction and siting** before adding sensors — the same conclusion as
   the microphone side, and for the same reason.
3. Only then consider a **3–4 element array** at 100–300 m aperture for local sources with azimuth.
4. Treat **microbarom stratospheric sounding as aspirational** unless you can site a ~1 km aperture array
   with serious wind-noise plumbing, or site near an existing reference array as NORSE did.

The one thing worth doing *early and cheaply*, if infrasound appeals: find out whether there's a **quarry
or mine with regular scheduled blasts** within a few hundred km. Known-source, known-time, repeatable
signals are the calibration dataset that makes everything else tractable — the infrasound equivalent of
what ADS-B does for the acoustic work.
